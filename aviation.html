Small Airports, Big Comeback

Did COVID-19 temporarily disrupt American air travel — or permanently change which airports matter?

  • 1,038 U.S. airports
  • 16 years of BTS T-100 records, 2009–2024
  • Python · pandas · SciPy · NetworkX
  • 2025

The question

Aviation theory says big hubs win permanently, because scale and connectivity compound: more routes attract more passengers, which fund more routes. COVID was the largest shock the system has ever absorbed.

When the hierarchy re-formed, did it snap back to where it was — or did it move?

What I did

Built a reproducible pipeline over sixteen years of the U.S. DOT’s T-100 Market filings — a monthly, carrier-by-route record of how many passengers flew between any two airports. Sixteen years of that is not an analysis dataset, and getting from one to the other was most of the work.

  • Consolidate
  • Drop cargo
  • Deduplicate
  • Domestic scheduled only
  • Annual roll-up per airport

The union of every origin and destination code gives 1,038 airports, each classified against FAA thresholds by its share of national enplanements — at least 1% major, 0.25% medium, 0.05% small, otherwise non-hub. On top of that sit three analyses: hub performance, the route network as a graph, and a constructed measure of why people were flying.

Worked alone, in Python. Every figure on this page comes out of the notebooks.

What happened

Small hubs recovered faster than major hubs, and the gradient runs cleanly down the hub classes. Comparing each airport’s 2023–24 passenger average against its own 2018–19 baseline:

+12.1% Small hubs median · n = 71
+4.5% Medium hubs median · n = 40
+1.3% Major hubs median · n = 29

A Kruskal–Wallis test across the three classes gives H = 11.69, p = 0.009. Mood’s median test was run separately to confirm the medians specifically rather than the distributions: p = 0.0082. In passenger terms the small-hub cohort went from an average of 114 million journeys a year to 158 million.

The decisions, and what each cost

Which years count as “after”?

Chose
Out of the sixteen-year record, compare a 2018–19 baseline directly against 2023–24 — and delete 2020–22 from the analysis entirely.
Because
The question is whether patterns changed permanently, not how bad the crash was. The shutdown years contain a 60% collapse that would dominate any trend line.
Cost
I see the endpoints but not the path — an airport that recovered in 2022 is indistinguishable from one still climbing in 2024. It also makes the result depend on 2024 being a settled year rather than a waypoint.

When should an airport’s hub class be fixed?

Chose
Compute it once, from each airport’s 2018 share of national enplanements, and hold it constant across both periods.
Because
This is the decision the whole study rests on. Recompute categories each period and an airport that grows enough gets promoted — its growth disappears into the definition instead of showing up in the result. Fixing the class at baseline is what makes “small hubs grew faster” a measurable statement rather than a tautology.
Cost
The FAA republishes classifications annually, so a 2018 snapshot disagrees with the official list for any airport near a threshold. I am measuring the cohort that was small in 2018, not what the FAA calls small today.

Parametric or non-parametric tests?

Chose
Run both, lead with the non-parametric results, and report medians rather than means as the headline.
Because
Shapiro–Wilk rejected normality and roughly 13% of airports were flagged as outliers by the IQR rule. That isn’t dirty data — growth rates are structurally right-skewed.
Cost
Twice as many numbers to explain, and the tests don’t answer identical questions. Means, distributions and medians can disagree.

How do you measure why someone flew?

Chose
Build a Travel Purpose Index — a 0–100 score per route-month weighting the destination city above the origin, scaled by classification confidence.
Because
T-100 records how many people flew, never why. No purpose field exists, so testing a leisure-versus-business hypothesis means constructing the variable or abandoning the question.
Cost
This is the weakest link in the project and I’d rather say so than bury it. The weights are hand-set — a ski destination gets −8 in winter, a state capital +8 year-round — chosen by me, not fitted to anything.

Not established

This is a two-period comparison, not a causal model. It shows that the hierarchy moved, not why.

Hub category explains under 1% of the variance in airport growth, so class membership is a weak predictor for any individual airport. Small hubs are only 8–10% of U.S. enplanements, making this a relative shift rather than a takeover. The Travel Purpose Index above is constructed, not measured. And the paper has not been accepted or published anywhere — nothing on this page should be read as peer-reviewed.
